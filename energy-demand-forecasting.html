PSCO Electricity Demand Forecasting

24-hour-ahead load forecasting and peak-demand analysis

An end-to-end forecasting workflow for the Public Service Company of Colorado balancing authority, built on six years of EIA hourly demand and NOAA weather data, and delivered as an operational Power BI report.

RoleSole data scientist
DataEIA-930 and NOAA, 2020 to 2025
ToolsPython, scikit-learn, LightGBM, XGBoost, Power BI
Result5.00% MAPE on unseen 2025 data

The business problem

An electric utility has to balance supply and demand continuously. Short-term load forecasts drive generation planning, resource scheduling, capacity management and peak-demand preparation, and the cost of being wrong runs in both directions: under-forecast the peak and reliability is at risk, over-forecast it and the utility pays for capacity it never uses.

The question this project set out to answer was specific: how much electricity should the PSCO system expect for each hour of the following day, and when is it most likely to peak?

That meant a 24-hour-ahead hourly forecast that beats a realistic operational benchmark, flags high-demand periods, quantifies its own error, explains what drives demand, and reaches stakeholders as something they can interrogate rather than a number handed over.

The data

Two public sources, both on real operational cadence.

  • Electricity demand from the EIA-930 dataset for the PSCO balancing authority, roughly 52,500 hourly observations spanning 2020 to 2025.
  • Weather from NOAA Local Climatological Data at Denver International Airport (station USW00003017): dry-bulb temperature, relative humidity, dew point and wind speed, chosen because consumption tracks heating and cooling need.

The EIA API caps how many observations a single request returns, so demand was pulled year by year with pagination and each year's row count checked against the API's own reported total, rather than assuming a download was complete.

Data quality, where most of the work was

The two sources came from different systems with different time conventions, and reconciling them honestly mattered more to the final result than model choice did.

  1. Source gaps, found rather than assumed. Comparing downloaded counts against API totals surfaced a roughly five-day hole in the 2023 demand series that the API itself returned short. Knowing it was a source gap and not a download failure changed how it was handled.
  2. Missing demand was excluded, not imputed. Demand is the target variable. Imputing it would have inflated apparent accuracy by scoring the model against numbers it had effectively been given.
  3. Timezone reconciliation. EIA timestamps were read as America/Denver, NOAA as fixed Mountain Standard Time; both were converted to UTC, merged on UTC, then converted back to Mountain Time for calendar analysis. Without that, weather and demand would have been joined hours apart for half the year.
  4. Daylight-saving ambiguity. A small number of transition-hour timestamps could not be resolved unambiguously and were dropped rather than guessed at.
  5. Weather gaps, handled by length. Around 140 merged rows arrived without weather. Gaps of three hours or less were interpolated; longer ones were not. A 2025 NOAA outage running about 91 consecutive hours was excluded outright, because interpolating four days of weather is inventing it.
  6. Anomalies flagged, not corrected. Negative, zero and extreme demand values were treated as source anomalies and removed from training rather than silently adjusted.

Feature engineering

Temperature in the training window ranged from about -22℉ to 101℉, so the features had to carry both winter heating and summer cooling response.

  • Calendar: hour, day of week, month, day of year, weekend and holiday indicators.
  • Cyclical: hour and day-of-year encoded as sine and cosine pairs, so hour 23 sits next to hour 0 and December next to January instead of at opposite ends of a number line.
  • Weather: raw temperature, humidity, dew point and wind speed, plus heating degree, cooling degree and temperature squared to capture the nonlinear response at extremes.
  • Historical demand: lags at 24, 48 and 168 hours, with 24-hour rolling mean and rolling max.

Two decisions here protected the result. Lags were built by timestamp lookup rather than row shifting, because the series contains genuine gaps and a row shift would have silently misaligned every subsequent value. And rolling features were anchored to the forecast origin, not the prediction timestamp, so a 24-hour-ahead forecast only ever saw information available 24 hours earlier. Without that anchor, future demand leaks into training and the model looks far better than it is.

Modeling strategy

Training ran on 2020 to 2023, validation on 2024, and final evaluation on 2025, held back untouched until model selection was finished. No random split was used: randomly shuffling a time series lets future observations inform past predictions, which is not a mistake a backtest recovers from.

Five approaches were compared, with a 24-hour persistence baseline kept throughout. Persistence predicts each hour using demand from exactly 24 hours earlier, and it is a genuinely strong predictor, which is what makes it the honest benchmark. A forecast that cannot beat yesterday is not worth operating.

Where the forecast is weakest

Average accuracy hides a great deal, so error was examined by hour, by month and by direction.

Peak-load prediction needed separate treatment. A model can post a strong overall MAPE while still underestimating extreme system demand, and for a utility the peak is the number that drives capacity decisions. The dashboard therefore scores peak magnitude and peak timing as their own measures rather than folding them into an average.

Reporting in Power BI

The 2025 forecast results were built into a three-page operational report.

  1. Executive forecast overview. MAPE, MAE, RMSE, baseline MAPE and relative error reduction as KPIs, with actual against predicted daily demand across 2025, filterable by date range, season and weekday or weekend, plus actual and forecast system peaks.
  2. Forecast performance and error analysis. MAPE by month, baseline against model, error by hour, actual against predicted, the error distribution, over- against under-forecasting, and peak-hour performance. This page exists to show where the forecast stops being reliable.
  3. Demand drivers and load patterns. Temperature against demand, average hourly load profiles, weekday against weekend, seasonal behaviour, lag comparisons, rolling demand and feature importance, so stakeholders can see why demand moves instead of being handed a black box.

What the analysis showed

  • Historical load is overwhelmingly the strongest predictor. Day-to-day and week-to-week persistence in electricity demand is high, which is exactly why the persistence baseline is hard to beat.
  • Weather is what produces the margin over that baseline, and it matters most during elevated heating or cooling periods.
  • Summer demand runs substantially higher, and the intraday profile repeats reliably enough to support load planning and operational staffing.
  • Strong average accuracy and good peak prediction are different problems and need to be measured separately.

Tradeoffs I made deliberately

  • Missing target demand excluded rather than imputed, to avoid fabricating the quantity being predicted.
  • Short weather gaps interpolated, long outages excluded, with the line drawn at three hours.
  • Timestamp-based lags over row shifting, because the series has real gaps.
  • Tree ensembles over deep learning. They matched the performance that mattered while staying fast to develop and straightforward to explain through feature importance, which the reporting depended on.
  • The persistence baseline kept in every comparison from start to finish.

Limitations

The backtest uses observed historical weather. A production 24-hour-ahead system would have to run on weather forecasts available at prediction time, which carry their own error, so these figures should be read as an upper bound on what the same features would deliver live.

Utility-specific inputs that would likely improve it further were out of reach in public data: customer-class demand, distributed solar generation, outages, demand-response events, pricing, industrial activity, major events and additional weather stations.

This is a rigorous historical forecasting framework rather than a productionised utility platform, and the distinction is worth stating plainly.

What I would do next

  • Swap observed weather for operational forecasts, and re-measure honestly against the degradation that causes.
  • Add prediction intervals so planners see uncertainty, not just a point estimate.
  • Train a dedicated peak-demand model rather than reading peaks off an hourly forecast.
  • Automate daily scoring with drift monitoring and a scheduled Power BI refresh.
  • Hyperparameter optimisation for the gradient-boosted models and time-series cross-validation, plus additional weather stations.
