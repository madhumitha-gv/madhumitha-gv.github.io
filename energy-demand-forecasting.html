Energy Demand Forecasting

Time series modeling on hourly demand and weather

A load-forecasting workflow across 50,000+ hourly demand and weather records, built to beat a baseline and to explain what drives the evening peak rather than only predicting it.

RoleSole data scientist
DomainEnergy, time series
ToolsPython, SQL, XGBoost, Power BI
Headline8.9% MAPE

The problem

A utility has to plan generation, purchasing and staffing around how much electricity its customers will use. Under-forecast the peak and reliability is at risk; over-forecast it and the company pays for capacity it never needs.

So the goal was two things at once: a forecast accurate enough to plan against, and a clear read on what actually drives the peak. A number on its own does not tell a planner what to do differently.

The data

  • 50,000+ hourly records of electricity demand, joined against weather observations for the same hours.
  • Queried and joined in SQL, then cleaned and explored in Python.

Approach

  1. Baseline first. Before any modeling, I set a simple baseline so every later model had a number it was required to beat. Without one, an impressive-looking error rate means nothing.
  2. Feature engineering. Temporal features covering calendar effects, time-of-day patterns and recent demand history, plus environmental features derived from the weather data.
  3. Modeling. Regression and XGBoost models trained and compared under time-aware validation, so the model never trains on hours that come after the ones it is tested on. Random splits leak the future into training and flatter the result.
  4. Reporting. Results published to Power BI dashboards covering peak-load drivers, demand trends and operational KPIs.

Results

MeasureResult
Forecast error (MAPE)8.9%
Error against baseline18% lower
Records modeled50,000+ hourly

What I took from it

  • Weather and time-of-day features carry most of the signal for peak demand.
  • The dashboard mattered as much as the model. Planners asked "why is the peak moving?" more often than "what is the peak?", and a forecast that cannot answer the first question gets ignored.

What I would do next

  • Add prediction intervals, so planners see the uncertainty around a forecast rather than a single point.
  • Retrain on a schedule and monitor for drift as seasonal weather patterns shift.
